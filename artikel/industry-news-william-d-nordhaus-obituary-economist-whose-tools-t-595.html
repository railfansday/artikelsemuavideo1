In a recent development, william (Bill) Nordhaus was an economist known for introducing climate change into economic analysis, earning him the 2018 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences. Profoundly curious and able to distill complex issues to their core, Nordhaus produced groundbreaking scholarship powered by his joy of discovery, dedication to getting things right and commitment to ensuring that science serves society.

His research shaped our understanding of what drives economic growth and innovation. He realized that omitting the environment from economic measurement would send the wrong signals about the directions of economic growth and, therefore, pioneered more-inclusive ways to measure growth.

Nordhaus was born in Albuquerque, New Mexico. His father developed a ski area to the east of the city, and as a child, Nordhaus tried to derive models of snowfall. He earned an undergraduate degree in economics from Yale University in New Haven, Connecticut, in 1963, before moving to the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in Cambridge, where he completed his PhD in 1967 under the supervision of future Nobel prizewinner Robert Solow. He then returned to Yale as an assistant professor and spent the rest of his academic career there.

Nordhaus rapidly made a name for himself by developing an early rigorous model for endogenous growth — that which occurs inside an economic system, rather than being driven by external factors. Before then, economic models had assumed that the rate of change in technology was independent of the economy.

It has been revealed that nordhaus’s PhD work showed that the pace of innovation depended on economic incentives, which could be altered by policy, such as through the patent system. His model also projected that research would face diminishing returns, with latest ideas getting harder to find — an idea that was validated 40 years later.

Industry observers note that next, he questioned the nature of economic growth. He recalled being inspired by an article in an in-flight magazine that described gross national product (GNP) as gross national pollution. In a 1972 article titled ‘Is Growth Obsolete?’, Nordhaus and economist James Tobin wrote that GNP exaggerates economic benefits by omitting environmental damage by “treating as free things which are not really free” and that “this practice gives the wrong signals for the directions of economic growth”. GNP also understates some of the benefits of economic growth, such as increased household well-being, from inventions such as electricity and home appliances.

This work alone gave rise to three influential lines of research: climate economics, the role of national accounts in measuring sustainable development and the importance of including new goods in measures of national income and economic well-being.

Nordhaus also considered the role of innovation. In a 1996 article on the history of the price of lighting, he measured the output and cost of various sources of light across human history (see go.nature.com/478rjyh). He showed that the light output per unit of energy of the compact fluorescent bulb was about 30,000 times greater than that of our distant ancestors’ fire.
