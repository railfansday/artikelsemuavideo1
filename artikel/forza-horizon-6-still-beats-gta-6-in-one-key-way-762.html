Forza Horizon 6 Still Beats GTA 6 in One Key Way

Recent findings suggest that rockstar Games is justifiably proud of everything it put into Grand Theft Auto 6, and one technical detail the studio has taken the time to highlight is the game's vehicle physics simulation. According to Ben Lyons, Rockstar's technical director for physics, some of the team started building and racing cars after finishing Grand Theft Auto 5, even winning in their class at the Nürbrugring 24 hours.

"This taught us a huge amount about how to modify, design, and set up real cars to go fast and what happens to them when things go wrong, like bursting tires and crashing," Lyons told Polygon in a statement. (As if to underline the point, Lyons' LinkedIn profile pic highlights him proudly holding up a blown racecar tire in a pit garage.) "We also took the same approach with rally-racing dirt bikes — it’s important to us to understand the difference between racing on two and four wheels and on- versus off-road so we can give our players as convincing authentic experience as we can," he stated. "For this game, we completely rewrote the vehicle simulation for every vehicle type so we could add more detail and realism, as well as authenticity and believability, to the game.".

Knowing how detail-obsessed Rockstar's developers are, and how seemingly limitless their resources were, I wondered if GTA 6's handling physics would be able to hold their own against the best the racing game genre has to offer​​​​​​​. My interest was doubly piqued when Polygon's reviewer Giovanni Colantonio told me there was a ton of racing in the game, both in the form of organized events on racing circuits and impromptu street races. Could GTA 6 secretly be a great racing game, along with everything else?

The answer is: no, not really. The cars are great to drive, and the races can be fun, but the design and technology of GTA 6 are fundamentally not set up for racing, and it shows.

Recent findings suggest that as a benchmark to measure GTA 6 against, I chose Forza Horizon 6. It wouldn't be right to compare Rockstar's everything-game to the most realistic circuit-racing sims, but an open-world racing game that has entertaining, accessible, and credible driving physics, with a degree of depth, seemed like fair game.

Sources indicate that GTA 6's racing options are hardly minor. Street-racing leagues unlock in chapter 3, but you can roll up to any circuit and start racing, or log some time trial laps, from the very beginning. A racing career is even a viable way to make money in the game. If you have enough cash for the entry fee and to rent a competitive ride (or you could just steal one), the payouts for top-three placements represent a decent profit.

It has been revealed that as a driving — as opposed to racing — game, there are a number of things that GTA 6 actually does better than Forza. For starters, there's the clearest fruit of Lyons' racing experience: the damage modelling. It's not as astounding as the soft-body physics in BeamNG.drive, but GTA 6 does have extremely detailed and reactive damage that meaningfully affects car handling and performance as well as smashing and peeling away body panels. Big plus.

In a recent development, I've already written about GTA 6's genius use of the PS5 DualSense controllers' adaptive triggers to allow for easy, relaxed driving at cruising speeds when exploring or just driving from place to place. That's something I wish Forza Horizon could have. And while Forza Horizon 6's Japan has more outright beautiful scenery than GTA 6's Leonida, the incredible richness and detail of Rockstar's game makes it an equal or greater pleasure to explore. The roads are much busier with traffic, all of which seems to be driven by reactive (if somewhat chaotic) humans rather than Forza's silent automatons. Also — not an insignificant detail when it comes to the cruising experience — the radio stations are much better.

Sources indicate that for sheer driving pleasure, I wouldn't put GTA 6 ahead of Forza, but it's not far behind. The rolling gait of its soft-suspension civilian rides makes them fun (if not easy) to chuck around, and drifts and tail-wagging burnouts are easy and satisfying to pull off. You get a good sense of the surface you're driving on when off-roading, and the world around you has much more credible and reactive physics than it does in Forza, including things like mud and street furniture (which, in Forza, all appears to be made from either balsa wood or the hardest substance known to man).

In a recent development, but anywhere where you care to examine the games' racing chops, Forza wins out. GTA 6's car customization has great cosmetic options, but the performance upgrades are rudimentary compared to Forza's. Forza has a manual transmission option, which, if you want to use it, grants much finer control over, and connection with, the car.

When you're actually racing, the gulf opens up. GTA's racing AI is in the spirit of the whole game's AI: it's chaotic, violent, and random. Races are tough to win because anything can happen, not because the racers are actually competitive. They're knockabout, almost slapstick affairs in which there's seldom any sign of anyone trying to follow a racing line. I've been in GTA 6 races with five or six cars driving abreast down the straight and then sideswiping each other on the corners. It's madness.

In the end, GTA 6's most profound failing as a racing game might also be its subtlest. It's the camera. It's not just that there's no in-car or first-person view (unless you're looking at your phone, which I do not recommend). Even the chase cameras, which look much like those of Forza Horizon 6, are just not as well tuned for racing. I'm not quite sure why — I think it has something to do with elevation and field of view, as well as the way the camera moves with the car as it turns — but it's much harder to pick and hold a line through a corner in GTA 6. You feel as though you're pushing your car around the circuit from above rather than sitting in it.

This effect isn't helped by the undefined track design, the handling model's tendency toward understeer, and the lack of audio and tactile feedback about where the edge of the car's grip is. Specifically when racing, GTA 6 feels woolly, where Forza is razor sharp.

This is a fundamentally unfair exercise; GTA 6 isn't a racing game, and it isn't really trying to be. As a driving game, it's hugely pleasurable, with a distinctive character and an amazing world to explore. The races are a fun diversion. But if you compare them to a true racer like Forza Horizon 6, you get a very swift and clear lesson in design intention. It doesn't matter how much money or detail went into GTA 6, or how good its physics model is; compared to something that was built for purpose, it ends up looking sloppy.
